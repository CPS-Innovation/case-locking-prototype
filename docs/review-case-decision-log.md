# Review a case: decision log for research

Important note: AI helped generate this from a 60 minute meeting. We’ve done some light edits. Anything that feels wrong or off, please let us know.

This is a write-up of a walkthrough of the "review a case" prototype flow, turned into something you can refer back to more easily than a video.

For each area of the flow, this notes what we've decided, why, and how solid that decision is:

- Decision: a choice we've made and the reasoning behind it
- Evidence-based: informed by prior research or testing, not just our judgement
- Assumption: untested, something we believe but haven't checked
- Open question: something we've not resolved yet

The aim is to make it clear what we already know versus what we're hoping your sessions will tell us, so you can plan the research around the gaps rather than the parts we're already fairly confident about.

## Overview page

Decision: the top three boxes (cases needing review, pending authorisation or with hearings needing preparation) are generated by the state machine work, showing a reviewing lawyer what's on their plate. Cases needing review is the main scenario we're testing. The state machine is not the main focus of the research, but we're keeping an eye on anything we can learn from it as we go.

Decision: each box is a shortcut into a pre-filtered case list, so users don't have to work out which filters to combine themselves.

Assumption: a user might bypass the overview page entirely, for example by going straight to the cases tab and searching for a specific case. We don't think that's a problem, since they'd still reach the same endpoint, but it's worth watching how people actually navigate rather than assuming they'll always start by using the overview boxes.

Decision: the "high priority" section (statutory time limit ending tomorrow, hearing this week) is a first stab at what counts as high priority. Not a focus for this research.

Decision: "recently viewed cases" is carried over from what exists in production today. Not a focus for this research.

## Case list

Decision: clicking "cases need review" lands on the case list pre-filtered to cases assigned to the signed-in user with review status of ‘review needed’. Search, sort and filter are available but shouldn't be needed for the main review scenario, since we're only interested in cases that have just come in and are not charged.

Decision: filters exist for priority and state-machine status, but we're not expecting users to need them for this scenario. Case statuses (not charged, charges pending, charged and the various end states) reflect background state-machine work and aren't the main research focus, though they may come up if participants ask.

## Case overview

Decision: the offence shown here is the one that came in from the police (actual bodily harm in the scenario we’ve created). There have been discussions about whether the police’s suggested offence should be shown here or somewhere else, but we've taken the view for now that since it's coming from the police we should show it. Users don't have to engage with it at this point, but it's there, and they'll see it again once they get into the review.

## Review task list

Decision: this uses the standard GOV.UK task list pattern, split into police report, witness statements, exhibits, previous convictions and other material, followed by an assessment and decisions section (case summary, information request, offences and elements strength assessment, charging decision).

Decision: police report and previous convictions each get their own single-item section, rather than being grouped with other material, because they’re important and the IDPC always has to include both.

Decision: the current section groupings mirror how material is organised in CMS today. This is a starting point, not necessarily final. There are other ways we could organise it in future, for example splitting exhibits into photographs, audio, video, etc.

Assumption: the task list pattern discourages tasks being blocked by other tasks, so right now nothing prevents someone opening the assessment and decision tasks before they've gone through the material. We assume users won't do this in practice, since there'd be nothing to base an assessment on, but we want to watch out for it in research. Ideally we'd like to avoid blocking tasks if we can.

Decision: "mark as completed" and "finish later" replace the standard GOV.UK task list radio buttons at the end of each task, and stay sticky as the user scrolls so they're always reachable. Marking a task as completed doesn't stop someone going back into it, it's just a marker. It will never lock a task, someone can always go back in and change something even after marking it as completed. The intention, once the whole thing is fully built out, is that it might work the other way round: completing all of the material review tasks could unlock tasks like offences and elements strength assessment and charging decision, rather than completing those tasks ever being blocked or locked. Not enforced in the prototype either way yet.

Assumption: "finish later" reflects that a review can take hours, days or even weeks for complex cases, and a lawyer might get interrupted and need to pick up where they left off. Functionally it behaves like the back link, marking the task as in progress, we've also considered calling it "mark as in progress" instead.

Assumption: we expect users will open multiple pieces of material at the same time to cross-reference, using native browser behaviour, right click, shift click or middle click to open in new tabs, rather than anything we've built specifically to support this. Worth watching how participants actually do this, if at all.

Decision: case summary is deliberately a single free-text task, standing in for roughly six or seven separate fields that exist in CMS today. We know there are things users need to record outside of linking evidence to elements of the charge, but modelling all of that was out of scope for now.

Assumption: we expect users may want to come back to case summary regularly throughout a review, possibly keeping it open in a separate tab or window as they work, rather than filling it in once. We don't know if that's how people will actually use it, or whether needing to open a task to get to it will be a usability problem. We may also find that in research users follow other approaches, such as writing into a Word document. This is fine and we can learn from it.

Decision: information requests (action plans) should not be needed for the scenario we're testing, but the journey exists in the prototype in case it comes up.

Decision: element strength assessment and the charging decision are tasks in themselves.

Decision: we considered adding a count of the number of notes to each task in the task list. We decided not to do this since in the current prototype, notes do not need to be converted into evidence or an anticipated issue. If they did then '5 notes' might encourage a user to go into a task to take action. Even then it may not be necessary, however, for example if we add a new task specifically for converting or deleting notes. That would prevent users from having to go into multiple tasks to deal with notes, and the count would be less useful.

## Materials: text documents

Decision: the materials themselves are real, anonymised cases with names, dates and details changed, but we've deliberately kept the documents largely as they'd appear in CMS today, including wording we wouldn't necessarily choose ourselves. This is to avoid introducing a debate about how documents should be worded into this round of research. Related, where a document has something like a field label followed by a name (for example "name of arresting officer:"), we're not surfacing that as structured data, it's just words in the document. That's a time constraint for getting into research, not a considered design decision.

Decision: selecting text reveals an in-context menu with options to mark as evidence, anticipated issue, information request note, note, or redact.

Decision: rather than taking users to a separate page to add a note, evidence tag, and so on, everything happens in place on the document, with the detail appearing on the right of the page. This mirrors the convention used by tools like Word, Google Docs and Dropbox Paper, where you select something and act on it in context. The alternative, a separate step-by-step flow with more room to explain each concept, would be more typical of a GOV.UK service for members of the public, but our hypothesis is that people reviewing a document want to see what they're annotating while they annotate it. This makes for a relatively complex interface by GOV.UK standards, and it's a deliberate trade-off we want to test.

Assumption: because this interaction pattern isn't explained anywhere, a user may not realise at first that selecting text reveals a menu. They may also not know what some of the menu options will do. We wouldn't consider that an outright usability failure on its own, given this is a repeat-use specialist tool rather than a one-off public-facing journey, and we're not relying on people knowing everything upfront. We do want to see how much people pick up naturally though, before they’re pointed in the right direction.

Decision: we've deliberately not built any onboarding or guided tour to help someone discover that selecting text reveals a menu. We considered it, for example a dismissible first-time walkthrough, but chose not to include it in this round because we want to see how well people get on with the interaction unprompted.

Decision: if the reviewing lawyer removes the offence from the case, the evidence and anticipated issue options disappear, since both are tied to elements of an offence. We're not expecting users to remove or change the offence in the research sessions and it should be discouraged.

Open question: watch for the thought process when someone considers whether the police-recorded offence should be something else. We've given one mechanism for changing it, but how people actually reason about this might change how we design it.

Evidence-based / assumption: our expectation is that people will add notes first while they're still working the case, then convert notes into evidence or an anticipated issue once things feel more settled, or delete the note if it turns out not to matter. This idea is based on reviewing lawyers not knowing what is evidence immediately, they’re building the case as they go. It's not purely usability testing here, it's also concept testing: does the note-first, convert-later model make sense to people?

Open question: do reviewing lawyers ever expect a note to just remain a note indefinitely, rather than eventually becoming evidence, becoming an anticipated issue, or being deleted?

Decision: information request note only captures a note at this point, not a full information request. Generating an actual request is a more complex journey we've deliberately kept out of this step, users pick the note up later in a separate task and turn it into a request. "Information request" is our attempt at plainer language than "action plan" (the term used in CMS), on the basis that a new user is unlikely to know either term, but is more likely to guess what "information request" means. Wording here isn't finalised and may still change before testing.

Assumption: it's not yet clear whether the two-step idea, add a note now, formalise it into a request later, will make sense to people, or whether they'll expect to do it in one step.

Decision: anticipated issue works the same way as evidence, just the "negative" equivalent, shown in red.

Evidence-based: the optional reasoning box, for saying why a particular selection of text was linked to an element, is informed by an earlier concept test of Rebekah's Claude mock-up, where something similar seemed to have value. It's optional, so people don't have to use it, but we think it helps avoid someone else (or the same lawyer, later) having to work out why particular elements/rationale were recorded.

## Materials: photos, audio and video

Decision: photos and audio work the same as text, with the same evidence, anticipated issue, information request note and note options, just applied to the whole item rather than a text selection. There's currently no redact option for photos, audio or video.

Decision: for audio, marking evidence against a specific point captures a timestamp, so anyone looking at it later can jump straight to the relevant moment. This currently only marks a single point, not a range.

## Case summary, information request and offences and elements strength assessment

Decision: information request, when someone chooses to request information, is a form flow following the standard GOV.UK pattern (rather than the in-context document pattern), since it isn't tied to a piece of material. It plays back any information request notes added earlier, asks whether to request information, and if so collects category, description and due date per item. This reflects the DCF model directly, we haven't designed the underlying structure ourselves.

Decision: offences and elements strength assessment is done as its own separate task, not inline within each piece of material. At one point we intended for this to be done within the materials pages, but following conversations with the wider team it was judged overly complex, so instead people build up evidence and anticipated issues while going through the material, then come to this task afterwards to assess each element's strength (strong, moderate or weak) with a rationale, seeing everything they've tagged for that element in one place.

Assumption: it's possible some participants won't have tagged much by the time they reach this task, in a 30-minute research session, which would leave little to work with here or at check details and submit. You may need to encourage participants to tag at least a little evidence and an anticipated issue as they go, so there's something to see later.

## Charging decision

Decision: this task plays back the elements and strengths just captured, since that's what should drive the decision. For research, participants should be steered toward the happy path of selecting "charge", even though other outcomes exist in the underlying model.

## Check answers and submit

Decision: task list items can be completed in any order, but the check answers page plays everything back in the same fixed order the tasks are presented in. We've discussed grouping this page differently, but are deliberately sticking with the standard pattern for now to see how well it works for users.

Assumption: we don't know yet whether users will expect a note to just stay as a note. It's flagged again here because the check answers page will show any notes that were never converted into evidence or an anticipated issue, and it isn't clear what people will expect to happen to them at this point. We won’t force them to convert or delete notes.

Decision: the text at the bottom of check answers now explicitly mentions the IDPC (previously it just said "we'll share it" more generally). This is a deliberate change to make the page specific to an initial review.

Open question: disclosure isn't handled in the prototype currently. We know it's a real consideration and may do more on it before research starts, but the current assumption is that the police report, previous convictions and anything tagged as evidence go into the IDPC. Disclosure isn’t dealt with but could be discussed with participants.

Open question: right now there's no manual step for a user to confirm what does or doesn't go into the IDPC, unlike CMS today. We've deliberately removed that step, on the basis that if something's already tagged as evidence, making someone confirm it again is redundant. It's worth watching for whether participants expect, or want, that manual confirmation step back, for example because something wasn't tagged as evidence but they think it should still be included in the IDPC.

## Post-submit case overview

Decision: after submitting, the case overview gains change links throughout (offences, case summary, and per-element strength, reason, evidence and witnesses), reflecting that a case isn't done in one sitting and may need revisiting as new information comes in. The grouping of evidence and witnesses under each element is taken from Rebekah's earlier mock-up.

Decision: a witness only appears in the submitted review's evidence and anticipated issues playback if evidence or an anticipated issue was tagged against their specific witness statement. If a participant's case doesn't show witnesses here, it's because nothing was tagged against a witness statement, not a bug.

## Other tabs (activity log, defendants, witnesses, hearings, information requests)

Decision: these tabs exist to show the case isn't a one-and-done interaction, and the activity log in particular shows the case's history (received, assigned, reviewed, awaiting charges). The scenario will not require participants to use these tabs. Change and delete links are present but not functional, this is out of scope for this round of work.

## Visual design

Decision: annotation cards use colour to distinguish notes, evidence, anticipated issues and information requests, which is a departure from our usual preference to avoid relying on colour. This isn't the only cue though, each card also has a text label, so colour isn't being used on its own to convey meaning.

## Moderator notes for testing the prototype

Use Marcus Webb for the primary review scenario, it's a not-charged, single-defendant, single-offence case with realistic material.

Use Daniel Palmer if you need a version of the same case that's been reviewed and completed. This may be useful for participants who don’t have time to do a full enough review themselves in a session. The lawyer notes for Daniel Palmer were generated by AI rather than written by us, and while they seem reasonable, they're there to be representative rather than a genuine legal assessment.

Some content/interaction is still in flux and may change before Friday’s research, including button labels (for example "finish later" was also considered as "mark as in progress") and terms like "information request note". Minor confusion about specific words in this round shouldn't be read as a strong signal on its own.
